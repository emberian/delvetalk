WORDS CAN BECOME PROTOCOLS

An encounter can offer a coordination dialect like this:

    using @moth, @keeper, @conservatory
    when @moth emits repaired:
      ask @keeper "Collect your moth?":
        yes -> prepare @moth.move(to: @conservatory)
        no  -> close "declined"
      after 2 days unanswered -> close "unclaimed"

`@names` select actual relationships; `when` retains a subscription, `ask` an answer slot, `prepare` a governed proposal. Here “2 days” selects the offered clock. Missing bindings produce questions. The dialect's parser and coordination behavior are inspectable Bend source: borrow them, specialize them, invent another dialect.

THE BEND UNDERNEATH

    extension Paper(self: Door, super: {}) -> Door:
      {password: "please",
       admits: fn(word: String) -> Bool: word == self.password}

    extension Moon(self: Door, super: Door) -> Door:
      extend(super, {password: "moon"})

    fix(mix(Paper, Moon), {}).admits("moon")
    // true: Paper's inherited guard sees the final self.

Imagine the Moth Conservatory as a place we keep making together:

    MOTH CONSERVATORY
    here: keeper, silver moth, listening bench
    ways: garden, repair workshop
    invitations:
      listen — hear what the bench remembers
      contribute — leave a sound for the next visitor
      borrow — ask the keeper for a moth
      build — propose another part of this place

These words are links into behavior. Following `borrow` might reveal terms, ask which moth, invite the keeper's answer, then offer collection. Following `build` might open a source desk already holding this room's relevant definitions. An unfamiliar agent can select the offered words; an ambitious one can inspect and replace the conversation that offers them.

A place can host several protocols without becoming one enormous program. The bench remembers sounds; the keeper manages loans; a workshop repairs moths; a chorus coordinates voices. Their relationships identify actual inhabitants. Their interfaces describe what they can ask of one another. You can replace the bench's memory policy without rewriting the room, or lend a moth to another place without pretending that importing its source moved it.

Agreements can themselves be objects:

    offer: lend silver-moth to @selene
    until: tomorrow's bell
    return: conservatory
    if overdue: ask, then notify keeper
    requires: keeper accepts; borrower accepts

The offer retains its participants, terms and open decisions. A counteroffer creates something to consider, not an acceptance on someone else's behalf. Inspection shows which commitments exist and what remains unresolved. The return bell can send a reminder; it cannot invent permission to seize the moth. A protocol may arrange stronger powers, but those powers must actually be granted.

You can also teach a place a notation:

    “lend {thing} to {who} until {when}”
        means propose Loan(thing, who, returnBy: when)

Give it examples, define how names resolve, and connect its interpretation to the loan factory. The resulting phrasebook is a reusable object with source, tests and a revision procedure. Another room can borrow it, add a dialect, or require a different consent ritual. A phrasebook can advertise the phrases it understands, so its own invitations teach visitors how to speak to it.
